Chapter 8

Sound

The music is sheet music played live by a sequencer on the DS's second processor. The voices are real recordings, more than forty thousand of them, squeezed into a codec built for phone calls. Both have to share sixteen hardware channels.

Two halves

Sound is split between the processors. The game, on the ARM9, decides what plays. It loads music and instruments into memory, then sends commands through the PXI queue (chapter 1) to the ARM7, which runs the sequencer and mixes the sixteen hardware channels. A command list is a chain of small records in shared memory; the ARM7 runs each list and counts it as done, and the game waits for that count when it needs to.

The sound archive

Music and effects live in one sound archive in Nintendo's standard format:

152sequences: the music, as notes and instrument changes
383instrument banks
832sampled waves the instruments play
5sequence archives of short sound effects and ambience

A sequence is like a MIDI file: tracks of notes, with tempo, volume, pan and pitch bends, played against a bank of instruments made of the archive's waves.

The sequencer

The ARM7's driver ticks about 192 times a second. Each tick, every playing sequence advances by its tempo, then each channel updates its envelope (attack, decay, sustain, release), its vibrato or tremolo, and its pitch slides. Volume is worked out in centibels: the player's volume, the track's, the note's velocity and the envelope are added up on a curved scale, then turned into a gain.

Sixteen channels are not many. When a sequence needs a channel and none is free, the one with the lowest priority loses, the quietest if there's a tie. Channels are searched in a fixed, odd-looking order (4, 5, 6, 7, 2, 0, 3, 1, then the rest), the same order the hardware driver uses.

What the game asks for

Scripts (chapter 3) play sound through a handful of commands: start a piece of music, play an effect, start or stop the ambience, play a voice clip. The game's sound functions add the rules a script never sees:

  • Music doesn't restart itself. Asking for the piece that's already playing does nothing. Two special numbers mean "this heroine's theme" and "the music for this mode".
  • Volume settings. Your music, sound and voice levels (0 to 15, saved in block A, chapter 6) scale each player.
  • Fades. A fade moves the volume one step per game frame, and loudness follows the square of the volume. A stop fades to silence, then ends the sequence.

GameSnd_PlaySeq is where music starts, and GameSnd_ApplyChannelVolume applies your settings.

The voices

Every voiced line is a clip in one 221 MB voice bank: 40,555 clips, with a table of where each starts and how long it is. They're compressed with Siren14, Polycom's codec for wideband phone calls (standardised as ITU-T G.722.1 Annex C): 20-millisecond frames of 40 bytes each, 16 kilobits a second, decoded to 32 kHz.

The game decodes voices itself, on the ARM9, with its own copy of the codec (the G7221_InitDecoder family). It decodes into a ring buffer, and an alarm fires each time a quarter of the ring has played, every 640 samples, one codec frame. The ARM7 plays the ring on two channels reserved for voice, so a line is never cut off by the music. Your voice volume setting is applied in decibels, with level 0 meaning silent.

The game measures the decoded voice's loudness as it goes, and that drives her mouth (chapter 5).

In lp-engine

The ARM7's program never runs. lp-engine has its own sound driver, written to the protocol the game speaks: it reads the same command lists, plays the sequences with its own sequencer, mixes the game's voice ring, and answers with the same alarms. Its sequencer was built from the ARM7 driver's own rules: envelopes, the volume curve, vibrato, channel stealing.

The voice decoder runs as the game's own code, exactly as on the DS. lp-engine also has a native Siren14 decoder, used by its tools, that produces identical output.

How we know

  • From power-on to the title screen, every instrument bank the driver received from the game is identical to the archive's, and every wave it plays decodes identically.
  • The voice the driver played at the title matches the clip decoded independently, sample for sample: one clip ran 153,658 samples before the game stopped it.
  • The sequencer's note stream matches a separate reference on all 151 playable sequences.
  • The voice volume curve was measured at all 16 levels in the real game and matches the formula 16 out of 16.

Not done yet. A few driver features the game hasn't been seen to use: capturing sound output, pulse and noise channels set up directly by the game, and surround output. The hardware's sample-and-hold sound isn't imitated either: the engine interpolates between samples, which sounds slightly cleaner. Details: docs/AUDIO.md and docs/RUNTIME.md.

How loud music plays

GameSnd_PlaySeq starts a sequence at (106 × the sequence's volume) ÷ 128, optionally fading it in. It then sets the player's volume from your setting: 105 × level ÷ 15. A fade moves the volume one step per game frame, and loudness follows (v ÷ 127)², so a linear fade sounds even. A stop fades to zero, then ends the sequence.

How loud she speaks

The voice level goes through three steps:

  1. The request's base value of 500 is scaled by your level (0 to 15) to 500 × (level + 15) ÷ 30, rounded down. Level 0 gives 0.
  2. GameSnd_ConvertVolumeToAttenuation turns that into centibels: (500 − value) × −640 ÷ 500.
  3. Anything at −640 or below is replaced with −1,024, which is silence.

That's linear in decibels: full volume at level 15, about 2.1 dB quieter per step, −29.9 dB at level 1, and silent at 0. It was measured at all 16 levels in the real game, and the formula matches every one.

Channels

Every sequence player may use any channel except 8 and 9, which the voice stream holds: the same mono samples on both, centred. Music and ambience share the other 14. When none is free, the driver steals the channel with the lowest priority, then the quietest. lp-engine plays music and ambience through one driver, so the stealing happens as it does on the DS.

The PXI protocol

Tag 7 carries the address of a chain of commands, each a "next" pointer, a command number and four arguments, or 0 for "run what you have". After each chain the driver increments a counter the game can wait on. Alarms come back over the same tag, and the driver renders the audio up to an alarm's due time before the game's handler runs, the same order as on the hardware. The game also reads the driver's state every update (players, tracks, channels), and lp-engine fills in the same layout.

The voice ring holds 2,560 16-bit samples per channel, played at period 0x200 (about 32,728 Hz). Its alarm fires every 640 samples, which is one codec frame and a quarter of the ring.
